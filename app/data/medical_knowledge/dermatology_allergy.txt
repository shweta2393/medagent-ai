DISEASE: Eczema (Atopic Dermatitis)
ICD10: L20.9 - Atopic dermatitis, unspecified; L20.89 - Other atopic dermatitis
CATEGORY: Dermatology
SYMPTOMS: Dry itchy skin (pruritus - hallmark), red to brownish-gray patches, small raised bumps that leak fluid when scratched (vesicles), thickened/cracked/scaly skin (lichenification from chronic scratching), raw sensitive swollen skin from scratching. Distribution: infants - face, scalp, extensor surfaces; children/adults - flexural areas (antecubital fossa, popliteal fossa), hands, feet, ankles, wrists, neck, upper chest, eyelids
RISK_FACTORS: Family history of atopic triad (eczema, allergies, asthma), filaggrin gene mutation (FLG - skin barrier defect, strongest genetic risk factor), childhood onset (90% by age 5), dry climate, urban environments, food allergies (milk, egg, peanut in children), environmental allergens (dust mites, pet dander), irritants (soaps, detergents, fragrances), stress, Staphylococcus aureus colonization (90% of AD skin)
DIAGNOSTIC_CRITERIA: Hanifin and Rajka criteria: Must have pruritus + >= 3 of: typical morphology and distribution, chronic/relapsing course, personal/family history of atopy, onset < 2 years. Severity scoring: SCORAD (SCORing Atopic Dermatitis) or EASI (Eczema Area and Severity Index). Mild (< 10% BSA), moderate (10-30% BSA), severe (> 30% BSA).
VITAL_SIGNS_INDICATORS: Normal vital signs, skin examination findings (distribution, morphology, excoriations), xerosis (dry skin), dermographism
LAB_INDICATORS: Elevated total IgE (in 80% of patients), peripheral eosinophilia (elevated eosinophil count), elevated allergen-specific IgE (food or aeroallergens), S. aureus on skin culture (colonization vs infection)
RECOMMENDED_TESTS: Clinical examination (primary - diagnosis is clinical), Skin prick testing or specific IgE (for allergen identification in moderate-severe), Complete blood count (eosinophilia), Total IgE level, Patch testing (if contact dermatitis suspected as trigger), Skin biopsy (if diagnosis uncertain - spongiotic dermatitis pattern), Skin culture (if superinfection suspected)
SPECIALIST: Dermatologist, Allergist/Immunologist, Pediatric Dermatologist
URGENCY: low (moderate if widespread superinfection - eczema herpeticum is a dermatologic emergency)
TREATMENT_OVERVIEW: AAD/EADV Guidelines: Foundational: moisturizers/emollients (applied immediately after bathing - cornerstone of treatment, liberal use TID-QID). Mild: low-potency topical corticosteroids (hydrocortisone 1-2.5%). Moderate: medium-potency TCS (triamcinolone 0.1%), topical calcineurin inhibitors (tacrolimus 0.03-0.1%, pimecrolimus 1%), PDE4 inhibitor (crisaborole 2%), JAK inhibitor (ruxolitinib cream). Severe: dupilumab (anti-IL4/IL13 - FDA approved 6 months+, first biologic for AD), tralokinumab (anti-IL13), oral JAK inhibitors (upadacitinib, abrocitinib), cyclosporine (bridge), phototherapy (NB-UVB). Avoid: topical steroids on face/skin folds long-term. Trigger avoidance, cotton clothing, fragrance-free products, lukewarm baths.
COMPLICATIONS: Skin infections (S. aureus impetigo, eczema herpeticum - HSV dissemination - emergency), sleep disturbance (significant impact on quality of life), mental health impact (anxiety, depression, social stigma), contact dermatitis from treatments, skin atrophy from overuse of potent TCS, atopic march (progression to allergic rhinitis and asthma)
WHO_GUIDELINE: WHO Essential Medicines includes hydrocortisone cream and emollients for dermatitis; WHO ICD-11 atopic dermatitis classification; WHO Global Burden of Disease ranks atopic dermatitis among the top 50 most prevalent diseases worldwide; affects ~15-20% of children globally
CDC_MAPPING: CDC NHIS data - eczema/atopic dermatitis affects ~31.6 million Americans (10.1%); prevalence has tripled in industrialized countries over the past 30 years; higher in children (13%) than adults (7.3%); significant racial disparities (higher in Black children); CDC tracks through National Health Interview Survey
PUBMED_REFERENCES: PMID:24813302 (AAD Atopic Dermatitis Guidelines 2014), PMID:28478972 (Dupilumab LIBERTY AD SOLO 1 & 2 Trials), PMID:33891299 (EADV/ETFAD 2021 AD Guidelines), PMID:34625408 (Abrocitinib JADE trials)
FOLLOW_UP_QUESTIONS: When did the rash first appear? Does it come and go? What makes the itching worse? Do you have a history of allergies, asthma, or hay fever? What skincare products do you use? Does anyone in your family have eczema? Does the itch affect your sleep?

DISEASE: Allergic Rhinitis (Hay Fever)
ICD10: J30.1 - Allergic rhinitis due to pollen; J30.2 - Other seasonal allergic rhinitis; J30.89 - Other allergic rhinitis; J30.9 - Allergic rhinitis, unspecified
CATEGORY: Allergy / Immunology / ENT
SYMPTOMS: Sneezing (paroxysms), runny nose (clear watery rhinorrhea), nasal congestion/obstruction, itchy nose/eyes/throat/ears/palate, watery red eyes (allergic conjunctivitis), postnasal drip, cough, facial pressure or pain (sinusitis overlap), reduced sense of smell (hyposmia), dark circles under eyes (allergic shiners - venous congestion), swollen eyelids, transverse nasal crease (allergic salute in children), pale/bluish boggy turbinates on exam, fatigue, mouth breathing, snoring
RISK_FACTORS: Family history of atopy (allergic rhinitis, asthma, eczema - strongest risk factor), other atopic conditions (atopic march), exposure to allergens: seasonal (tree, grass, weed pollen) or perennial (dust mites, pet dander, mold, cockroach), male sex in childhood (equal in adults), urban environments, high socioeconomic status (hygiene hypothesis), early antibiotic use, formula feeding
DIAGNOSTIC_CRITERIA: ARIA (Allergic Rhinitis and its Impact on Asthma) guidelines classification: Intermittent (< 4 days/week or < 4 weeks) vs Persistent (> 4 days/week and > 4 weeks). Severity: Mild (normal sleep, daily activities, school/work) vs Moderate-Severe (impaired). Diagnosis: clinical history (seasonal/perennial pattern) + positive allergen-specific IgE (skin prick test or serum) confirming sensitization matching clinical symptoms.
VITAL_SIGNS_INDICATORS: Normal vital signs, nasal mucosal findings (pale/boggy edematous turbinates, clear watery discharge), allergic shiners, conjunctival injection, Dennie-Morgan lines
LAB_INDICATORS: Positive skin prick test (wheal >= 3mm > negative control), elevated allergen-specific IgE (RAST/ImmunoCAP), elevated total IgE, nasal cytology showing eosinophils (> 10%), peripheral eosinophilia, normal CRP/ESR (distinguishes from infectious rhinosinusitis)
RECOMMENDED_TESTS: Skin prick testing (preferred, immediate results, cost-effective), Serum specific IgE (ImmunoCAP - when skin testing not feasible), Total IgE, Complete blood count (eosinophilia), Nasal endoscopy (if polyps/structural issues suspected), CT sinuses (if chronic sinusitis suspected), Peak nasal inspiratory flow (PNIF), Spirometry (if asthma comorbidity suspected), Component-resolved diagnostics (CRD - for immunotherapy selection)
SPECIALIST: Allergist/Immunologist, ENT/Otolaryngologist
URGENCY: low
TREATMENT_OVERVIEW: ARIA/AAAAI/ACAAI Guidelines: Step 1 (mild intermittent): allergen avoidance + oral antihistamines (cetirizine, loratadine, fexofenadine - second-gen non-sedating). Step 2 (moderate-severe/persistent): intranasal corticosteroids (fluticasone propionate, mometasone, budesonide - MOST EFFECTIVE single therapy), combination INCS + intranasal antihistamine (azelastine-fluticasone/Dymista - superior to either alone). Add-on: nasal saline irrigation (high-volume), oral antihistamines, leukotriene receptor antagonists (montelukast - especially with comorbid asthma, FDA black box warning for neuropsychiatric events), decongestants (short-term < 3 days only - rhinitis medicamentosa). Step 3 (refractory or allergen immunotherapy candidate): subcutaneous immunotherapy (SCIT - 3-5 years, disease-modifying) or sublingual immunotherapy (SLIT - tablets for grass, ragweed, dust mite). Anti-IgE (omalizumab) for severe refractory.
COMPLICATIONS: Chronic sinusitis, nasal polyposis, otitis media with effusion, sleep disturbance/OSA, impaired cognition/school/work performance, reduced quality of life (comparable to mild asthma), asthma exacerbation (united airway concept - 40% AR have asthma, 80% asthma have AR), oral allergy syndrome (pollen-food cross-reactivity)
WHO_GUIDELINE: WHO ARIA guideline update (Allergic Rhinitis and its Impact on Asthma) - integrated management of rhinitis and asthma; WHO Essential Medicines includes cetirizine, beclometasone nasal spray; WHO recognizes AR as the most common allergic disease globally affecting 10-30% of adults worldwide
CDC_MAPPING: CDC NHIS data - allergic rhinitis affects ~19.2 million US adults (7.7%) and 5.2 million children (7.2%); seasonal allergies reported by 25.7% of adults; significant impact on productivity (3.5 million lost workdays annually); CDC tracks allergies through NHIS and NHANES
PUBMED_REFERENCES: PMID:29353604 (ARIA 2019 Guideline Update), PMID:27707680 (AAAAI/ACAAI Joint Practice Parameter for Rhinitis 2017), PMID:24080990 (Allergen immunotherapy systematic review), PMID:26718692 (INCS vs antihistamines meta-analysis)
FOLLOW_UP_QUESTIONS: Are your symptoms seasonal or year-round? What triggers seem to worsen your symptoms? Do you have pets? Do you have asthma or eczema? Have you tried any allergy medications? Is your home well-ventilated or do you have carpeting? Do your symptoms affect your sleep or work performance?

DISEASE: Anaphylaxis
ICD10: T78.2XXA - Anaphylactic shock, unspecified, initial encounter; T78.00XA - Anaphylactic reaction due to unspecified food, initial encounter; T88.6XXA - Anaphylactic reaction due to adverse effect of correct drug or medicament
CATEGORY: Allergy / Immunology / Emergency Medicine
SYMPTOMS: Rapid onset (minutes to hours after exposure) of multi-system involvement: SKIN (90%): urticaria (hives), pruritus (itching), flushing, angioedema (lip/tongue/throat/periorbital swelling). RESPIRATORY (70%): dyspnea, wheezing, stridor, throat tightness, hoarseness, cough, nasal congestion. CARDIOVASCULAR (45%): tachycardia, hypotension, dizziness, syncope, cardiac arrest. GI (45%): nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, abdominal cramps, dysphagia. CNS: anxiety, sense of impending doom, confusion, loss of consciousness
RISK_FACTORS: Previous anaphylaxis episode (strongest predictor of recurrence), known allergies: food (peanut, tree nuts, shellfish, fish, milk, egg - most common triggers in children), medications (antibiotics - penicillin, NSAIDs, neuromuscular blocking agents), insect stings (Hymenoptera - bee, wasp, fire ant), latex, exercise-induced (food-dependent exercise-induced anaphylaxis), comorbid asthma (risk factor for fatal anaphylaxis), mastocytosis/elevated baseline tryptase, alpha-gal syndrome (red meat allergy - tick-mediated), concurrent ACE inhibitor or beta-blocker use (impairs epinephrine response)
DIAGNOSTIC_CRITERIA: NIAID/FAAN Clinical Criteria (2006, updated WAO 2020): Acute onset (minutes-hours) with involvement of >= 2 organ systems: skin/mucosal tissue AND respiratory compromise AND/OR reduced BP/end-organ dysfunction. OR: Acute onset after exposure to KNOWN allergen with reduced BP (adults: systolic < 90 mmHg or > 30% decrease from baseline). Serum tryptase elevation (drawn 15 min to 3 hours after onset, compare to baseline at 24+ hours; > 1.2x baseline + 2 ng/mL is significant).
VITAL_SIGNS_INDICATORS: Tachycardia (early compensatory), hypotension (systolic < 90 mmHg - anaphylactic shock), tachypnea, stridor, wheezing, urticaria, angioedema, SpO2 desaturation, weak/absent pulse (cardiac arrest)
LAB_INDICATORS: Serum tryptase elevated (> 11.4 ng/mL or > 1.2x baseline + 2) - peak 1-2 hours post-onset. Histamine levels elevated (within 15-60 minutes, degrades rapidly). ABG showing respiratory/metabolic acidosis in severe cases. Allergen-specific IgE (drawn 4-6 weeks post-episode for trigger identification).
RECOMMENDED_TESTS: Serum tryptase (drawn within 1-3 hours of onset + repeat at 24+ hours for baseline comparison), Complete blood count (eosinophilia), Metabolic panel, Specific IgE testing (serum, 4-6 weeks after episode to identify trigger), Skin prick testing (4-6 weeks post-episode, by allergist), Component-resolved diagnostics (CRD - identify specific protein triggers), Baseline tryptase (if mastocytosis suspected), ECG (if cardiovascular symptoms)
SPECIALIST: Allergist/Immunologist (post-episode evaluation, mandatory referral), Emergency Medicine (acute management), Critical Care/ICU (refractory anaphylaxis)
URGENCY: critical (life-threatening emergency - biphasic mortality risk, death can occur within minutes)
TREATMENT_OVERVIEW: AAAAI/ACAAI/WAO Anaphylaxis Guidelines: FIRST-LINE (NO DELAY): Intramuscular epinephrine (anterolateral thigh) - 0.3-0.5mg for adults (1:1000 concentration), 0.01mg/kg for children (max 0.3mg). REPEAT every 5-15 minutes if no improvement. Call emergency services/activate code. Position: supine with legs elevated (Trendelenburg) unless respiratory distress. Supplemental high-flow O2. IV access + rapid IV crystalloid bolus (NS 1-2 liters for adults, 20mL/kg for children) for hypotension. SECOND-LINE: H1-antihistamine (diphenhydramine 25-50mg IV), H2-blocker (ranitidine 50mg IV or famotidine 20mg IV), nebulized albuterol (bronchospasm), corticosteroids (methylprednisolone 1-2mg/kg IV - may reduce biphasic reaction). Refractory: epinephrine infusion, vasopressors (norepinephrine), glucagon (if on beta-blockers). Observation: minimum 4-6 hours (up to 24 hours if severe, risk of biphasic reaction 1-20%). DISCHARGE: prescribe epinephrine auto-injector (EpiPen), anaphylaxis action plan, MedicAlert bracelet, MANDATORY allergist/immunologist referral.
COMPLICATIONS: Death (case fatality rate 0.7-2% of anaphylaxis episodes), biphasic anaphylaxis (recurrence 1-72 hours later in 1-20%), cardiac arrest, respiratory failure, permanent brain injury from prolonged hypoxia, protracted anaphylaxis (lasting hours-days despite treatment), psychological impact (PTSD, anxiety)
WHO_GUIDELINE: WHO Essential Medicines includes epinephrine (adrenaline) on Priority List; WAO (World Allergy Organization) Anaphylaxis Guidelines 2020 endorsed by WHO; WHO estimates anaphylaxis incidence 50-112 episodes per 100,000 person-years globally; WHO recommends availability of epinephrine auto-injectors in all healthcare settings
CDC_MAPPING: CDC/NCHS data - anaphylaxis-related ED visits in US estimated at 500,000-1 million annually; food allergy anaphylaxis increasing (estimated 200,000 ED visits/year); drug-induced anaphylaxis accounts for most fatal cases; CDC reports 1,500 deaths from anaphylaxis annually in US; CDC food allergy prevalence: 8% children, 10% adults
PUBMED_REFERENCES: PMID:32709527 (WAO 2020 Anaphylaxis Guidelines), PMID:25583912 (NIAID/FAAN Anaphylaxis Criteria), PMID:33741520 (AAAAI/ACAAI Anaphylaxis Practice Parameter 2020), PMID:29406333 (Epinephrine in anaphylaxis systematic review)
FOLLOW_UP_QUESTIONS: What did you eat, take, or come in contact with before this started? How quickly did symptoms develop after exposure? Have you had a similar reaction before? Do you carry an epinephrine auto-injector? Do you have known allergies? Do you have asthma? Are you taking beta-blockers or ACE inhibitors?
