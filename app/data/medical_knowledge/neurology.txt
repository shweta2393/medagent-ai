DISEASE: Migraine
ICD10: G43.909 - Migraine, unspecified, not intractable, without status migrainosus; G43.109 - Migraine with aura; G43.709 - Chronic migraine without aura
CATEGORY: Neurology
SYMPTOMS: Intense throbbing/pulsating headache (usually unilateral - 60%), nausea, vomiting, sensitivity to light (photophobia), sensitivity to sound (phonophobia), aura (visual disturbances: scintillating scotoma, fortification spectra, zigzag lines, temporary vision loss; sensory: tingling; speech: aphasia), fatigue/prodrome (mood changes, food cravings, neck stiffness 24-48 hours before), postdrome (fatigue, cognitive difficulty)
RISK_FACTORS: Female sex (3x more common), family history (strong genetic component), age 15-55, hormonal changes (menstruation, pregnancy, menopause, OCP), stress, certain foods (aged cheese, red wine, chocolate, MSG, nitrates), sleep changes (too much or too little), strong sensory stimuli, weather/barometric pressure changes, medications (vasodilators, OCP)
DIAGNOSTIC_CRITERIA: ICHD-3 criteria: At least 5 attacks lasting 4-72 hours (untreated) with at least 2 of: unilateral location, pulsating quality, moderate-to-severe intensity, aggravation by routine physical activity. Plus at least 1 of: nausea and/or vomiting, photophobia AND phonophobia. Migraine with aura: at least 2 attacks with reversible visual/sensory/speech symptoms developing over >= 5 minutes and lasting 5-60 minutes.
VITAL_SIGNS_INDICATORS: Generally normal between attacks, may have elevated blood pressure during acute attack, cranial autonomic symptoms (lacrimation, nasal congestion)
LAB_INDICATORS: Generally normal; labs used to exclude secondary causes (CBC, ESR, CRP, metabolic panel). No specific biomarker for migraine.
RECOMMENDED_TESTS: Neurological examination (must be normal for primary migraine), Brain MRI with and without contrast (to rule out secondary causes if atypical features, first severe headache, or neurological deficits), CT head without contrast (if sudden severe headache - "thunderclap" to rule out SAH), Complete blood count, ESR/CRP (temporal arteritis screening in patients > 50)
SPECIALIST: Neurologist, Headache Specialist
URGENCY: low to moderate (high if thunderclap headache, worst headache of life, neurological deficits, fever, or papilledema)
TREATMENT_OVERVIEW: AAN/AHS Guidelines: Acute treatment - triptans (sumatriptan first-line), NSAIDs, gepants (ubrogepant, rimegepant), ditans (lasmiditan), antiemetics (metoclopramide). Preventive (>= 4 headache days/month): beta-blockers (propranolol, metoprolol), topiramate, valproate, amitriptyline, CGRP monoclonal antibodies (erenumab, fremanezumab, galcanezumab, eptinezumab), onabotulinumtoxinA (chronic migraine >= 15 days/month). Neuromodulation devices (sTMS, Cefaly).
COMPLICATIONS: Status migrainosus (> 72 hours), migrainous infarction (stroke during aura), chronic migraine transformation, medication overuse headache (MOH), reduced quality of life, disability (2nd leading cause of disability globally per GBD study)
WHO_GUIDELINE: WHO Global Burden of Disease Study ranks migraine as 2nd leading cause of years lived with disability (YLD) globally; WHO Essential Medicines includes sumatriptan, ibuprofen, paracetamol for acute migraine; WHO Lifting the Burden campaign for headache disorders
CDC_MAPPING: CDC NHIS data - migraine affects ~15.3% of US adults (39 million); higher in women (20.7%) vs men (9.7%); annual cost estimated at $36 billion from lost productivity and healthcare
PUBMED_REFERENCES: PMID:29691012 (AAN/AHS Preventive Migraine Treatment Guideline), PMID:28837707 (ICHD-3 Classification), PMID:28002174 (Erenumab phase 3 STRIVE Trial), PMID:31291516 (CGRP antibodies systematic review)
FOLLOW_UP_QUESTIONS: How often do you get headaches? How long do they last? Is the pain on one side of the head? Do you experience any visual disturbances before the headache? What makes the headache better or worse? Do you have a family history of migraines? How many headache days per month?

DISEASE: Stroke (Cerebrovascular Accident - CVA)
ICD10: I63.9 - Cerebral infarction, unspecified (ischemic); I61.9 - Nontraumatic intracerebral hemorrhage, unspecified; I60.9 - Nontraumatic subarachnoid hemorrhage, unspecified; G45.9 - Transient cerebral ischemic attack (TIA)
CATEGORY: Neurology / Emergency Medicine
SYMPTOMS: Sudden numbness or weakness (face, arm, leg - especially one side), sudden confusion, trouble speaking or understanding speech (aphasia), sudden vision problems (homonymous hemianopia, monocular vision loss), sudden difficulty walking/dizziness/loss of balance/coordination (ataxia), sudden severe headache with no known cause (hemorrhagic), facial drooping, arm drift
RISK_FACTORS: Hypertension (most important modifiable risk factor - attributable risk ~50%), atrial fibrillation (5x stroke risk), diabetes, high cholesterol/dyslipidemia, smoking, obesity, history of TIA (90-day stroke risk 10-20%), age over 55, family history, carotid stenosis, sickle cell disease, excessive alcohol, illicit drugs (cocaine), OCP use in women who smoke
DIAGNOSTIC_CRITERIA: FAST assessment (Face drooping, Arm weakness, Speech difficulty, Time to call emergency). NIHSS (National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale) for severity. Non-contrast CT head (immediate - to rule out hemorrhage). CT angiography (CTA for large vessel occlusion). MRI DWI (most sensitive for acute ischemia). Time window: tPA within 4.5 hours, thrombectomy within 24 hours (DAWN/DEFUSE criteria).
VITAL_SIGNS_INDICATORS: Hypertension (often markedly elevated > 180/120, permissive hypertension initially), irregular heart rate (AFib), neurological deficits (NIHSS score), altered consciousness
LAB_INDICATORS: Elevated glucose (hyperglycemia worsens outcome), abnormal coagulation studies (if on anticoagulants), elevated troponin (cardiac comorbidity), lipid panel abnormalities, elevated HbA1c
RECOMMENDED_TESTS: CT head without contrast (emergent - door to CT < 25 minutes), CT angiography (head and neck), MRI brain with DWI/FLAIR, ECG (detect AFib), Echocardiogram (TTE/TEE for cardioembolic source), Carotid ultrasound/Doppler, Complete blood count, Coagulation studies (PT/INR, PTT), Lipid panel, Metabolic panel, HbA1c, Holter/event monitor (occult AFib)
SPECIALIST: Neurologist (Vascular), Neurosurgeon (hemorrhagic), Interventional Neuroradiologist, Stroke Team
URGENCY: critical (time is brain - 1.9 million neurons lost per minute of untreated large vessel occlusion)
TREATMENT_OVERVIEW: AHA/ASA Guidelines: Ischemic stroke - IV alteplase (tPA) within 4.5 hours of symptom onset, mechanical thrombectomy within 24 hours for large vessel occlusion (LVO) meeting imaging criteria, aspirin within 24-48 hours, blood pressure management (permissive hypertension unless > 220/120 or tPA given). Hemorrhagic - blood pressure control (target < 140 systolic), reversal of anticoagulation (idarucizumab for dabigatran, andexanet for Xa inhibitors, 4-factor PCC for warfarin), surgical evacuation if indicated. Post-stroke: rehabilitation, secondary prevention (antiplatelet, statin, BP control, carotid endarterectomy if > 70% stenosis).
COMPLICATIONS: Permanent disability (leading cause of long-term disability), cognitive impairment/vascular dementia, speech problems (aphasia), dysphagia/aspiration pneumonia, depression (30-50%), DVT/PE, recurrent stroke (annual risk ~5%), hemorrhagic transformation, death (5th leading cause in US)
WHO_GUIDELINE: WHO HEARTS technical package for cardiovascular disease management includes stroke prevention through hypertension control; WHO estimates 12.2 million new strokes annually worldwide (Global Burden of Disease 2019); WHO Essential Medicines includes alteplase, aspirin for stroke management
CDC_MAPPING: CDC Stroke Facts - stroke is 5th leading cause of death in US (~150,000 deaths/year); someone has a stroke every 40 seconds in the US; stroke costs ~$56.5 billion annually; disparities in Black/African American populations (2x risk)
PUBMED_REFERENCES: PMID:30879893 (2019 AHA/ASA Acute Ischemic Stroke Guideline), PMID:25671797 (MR CLEAN Trial - thrombectomy), PMID:29766772 (DAWN Trial - extended window thrombectomy), PMID:28535757 (2017 AHA/ASA Secondary Stroke Prevention)
FOLLOW_UP_QUESTIONS: When did the symptoms start exactly? Is the weakness on one side of the body? Can you smile evenly? Is there any difficulty speaking? Do you take blood thinners? Do you have atrial fibrillation or high blood pressure?

DISEASE: Meningitis
ICD10: G03.9 - Meningitis, unspecified; G00.9 - Bacterial meningitis, unspecified; A87.9 - Viral meningitis, unspecified
CATEGORY: Neurology / Infectious Disease / Emergency Medicine
SYMPTOMS: Severe headache (worst headache of life), stiff neck (nuchal rigidity), high fever, sensitivity to light (photophobia), nausea, vomiting, confusion/altered mental status, seizures, rash (petechial/purpuric in meningococcal - non-blanching), drowsiness, difficulty waking, bulging fontanelle (in infants), Kernig's sign positive (pain on knee extension with hip flexed), Brudzinski's sign positive (involuntary hip/knee flexion on neck flexion)
RISK_FACTORS: Age (neonates, adolescents 15-19, elderly > 60), living in close quarters (dormitories, military barracks), weakened immune system (HIV, asplenia, complement deficiency), not being vaccinated (MenACWY, PCV13, Hib), recent head surgery or skull fracture, cochlear implants, CSF shunts, travel to meningitis belt (sub-Saharan Africa)
DIAGNOSTIC_CRITERIA: Lumbar puncture CSF analysis is diagnostic: Bacterial - neutrophilic pleocytosis (WBC > 1000), low glucose (< 40 mg/dL or CSF/serum ratio < 0.4), high protein (> 200 mg/dL), positive Gram stain/culture. Viral - lymphocytic pleocytosis (WBC 10-500), normal glucose, mildly elevated protein, negative culture. Clinical triad: fever + headache + neck stiffness (sensitivity ~44% for full triad, individual components more sensitive). Jolt accentuation test.
VITAL_SIGNS_INDICATORS: High fever (> 101.3°F/38.5°C), tachycardia, hypotension (septic shock in severe bacterial), altered consciousness (GCS < 15), purpuric rash (meningococcemia)
LAB_INDICATORS: Elevated WBC with left shift, elevated CRP (> 20 mg/L) and procalcitonin (> 0.5 ng/mL strongly suggests bacterial), blood cultures positive (50-80% in bacterial meningitis), CSF analysis abnormal (see above), elevated lactate > 3.5 mmol/L in CSF (bacterial)
RECOMMENDED_TESTS: Lumbar puncture with CSF analysis (cell count, differential, glucose, protein, Gram stain, culture, latex agglutination), Blood cultures (x2 before antibiotics), Complete blood count with differential, CRP and Procalcitonin, CT head (BEFORE LP if any of: immunocompromised, history of CNS disease, new onset seizure, papilledema, altered consciousness, focal neurological deficit), Coagulation studies, Metabolic panel, CSF PCR panel (FilmArray - 14 pathogens), CSF HSV PCR
SPECIALIST: Infectious Disease Specialist, Neurologist, Critical Care/ICU, Emergency Medicine
URGENCY: critical (mortality 10-30% for bacterial meningitis even with treatment; antibiotics within 1 hour of suspicion - door-to-needle time)
TREATMENT_OVERVIEW: IDSA Guidelines: Empiric IV antibiotics IMMEDIATELY (do NOT delay for LP/CT): Neonates: ampicillin + cefotaxime. Children/Adults: ceftriaxone + vancomycin (± ampicillin if > 50 years or immunocompromised for Listeria coverage). Dexamethasone BEFORE or WITH first antibiotic dose (reduces mortality and neurological sequelae in pneumococcal meningitis). Viral: supportive care; IV acyclovir if HSV encephalitis suspected. Close contact prophylaxis: rifampin or ciprofloxacin or ceftriaxone for meningococcal.
COMPLICATIONS: Brain damage, hearing loss (10-30% in bacterial), cranial nerve palsies, hydrocephalus, cerebral edema, subdural effusion/empyema, learning disabilities, kidney failure, shock, limb amputation (meningococcal septicemia/DIC), death (10-30% bacterial, higher in neonates)
WHO_GUIDELINE: WHO Defeating Meningitis by 2030 Global Roadmap; WHO recommends MenA conjugate vaccine (MenAfriVac) in African meningitis belt; WHO Essential Medicines includes ceftriaxone, ampicillin, dexamethasone for meningitis; WHO estimates ~250,000 deaths from meningitis annually globally
CDC_MAPPING: CDC meningitis surveillance - ~1,200 cases of bacterial meningitis annually in US; case fatality rate 10-15%; CDC recommends MenACWY vaccine for all adolescents at 11-12 years with booster at 16; MenB vaccine for high-risk individuals and outbreaks
PUBMED_REFERENCES: PMID:15028823 (IDSA Bacterial Meningitis Guideline 2004 - still referenced), PMID:27706895 (Meningitis clinical review - Lancet), PMID:12093357 (Dexamethasone in bacterial meningitis - de Gans trial)
FOLLOW_UP_QUESTIONS: How quickly did the symptoms develop? Can you touch your chin to your chest? Have you been in close contact with anyone who was ill? Have you had any recent ear infections or sinus infections? Are your vaccinations up to date? Have you noticed any rash?
